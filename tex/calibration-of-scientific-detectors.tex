\section{Introduction}

For any serious imaging application based on empirical data, proper calibration
of the detector is necessary to obtain an unbiased estimator of the light level
seen by each pixel and the corresponding variance or precision.

Note: other approaches for considering non-uniform noise level: Anscombe
transform, exact Gaussian + Poissonian PDF, pragmatic models (like the
proposed Gaussian approximation):

\begin{itemize}
\item \citet{Foi_et_al-2008-Poissonian_Gaussian_noise_model_fitting} proposed a
method to derive the detector parameters as well as the clipping levels of the
analog to digital converter.  They consider a mixture of a Poissonian signal
dependent noise and a Gaussian signal independent noise described by two
parameters analogous to the gain\footnote{which they wrongly combine with the
quantum efficiency} and the variance of the readout noise (no zero-level
parameter) and account for clipping of values. Assuming that the model
parameters and the clipping levels are the same for all the pixels, they propose
a method to estimate all these parameters from a single calibration image of a
sufficiently \emph{smooth} object.  Like us they approximate the statistics of
the measured pixels by an independent heteroskedastic\footnote{with non-uniform
parameters} Gaussian distribution. Unlike us, they assume that all pixels have
the same parameters and do not consider defective pixels.

\item \citet{Jezierska_et_al-2011-Poisson_Gaussian_noise} proposed an EM
  algorithm to fit the parameters of a mixture of Poissonian and Gaussian
  noise.

\end{itemize}

The paper is organized as follows. We first show that accounting for the analog
to digital converter yields subtle digitization effects that rule out the most
elaborate statistics developed to describe the data delivered by a detector.
We therefore advocate that a simple non-homogeneous Gaussian approximation is a
good enough approximation. We then propose a global approach to jointly fit the
pixelwise parameters of the detector model on all relevant calibration data.




\section{Modeling the detector}
\label{sec:detector-model}

We consider imaging at optical wavelengths (visible or infrared) with a CMOS or
a CCD device.


\subsection{Principle of photon detection}
\label{sec:detection-principle}

Light detection is based on the photo-electric effect that occurs in the
metal-oxide-semiconductor of which the sensor is made. The photo-electric
effect converts a photon into an electron which is captured by the well of the
pixel.  For each incident photon, the probability of success of this process is
called the \emph{quantum efficiency} of the pixel.  During the exposure,
thermal jittering of the electrons in the substrate may cause some electrons to
overcome the potential barrier of the well and be captured.  The number of such
captured thermal electrons grows linearly with the time\footnote{provided the
  amount of captured electrons is negligible compared to the total number of
  electrons in the substrate and the full-well capacity is not exceeded}, hence
the name of \emph{dark current} for this contribution to the signal. During the
reading, the number of captured electrons is converted to a digital number in
so-called \emph{Analog-Digital Units} (ADU) which is an integer encoded in a
given number of bits.  The counting of the number of electrons is not perfect
and introduces so called \emph{readout} noise.  In addition to the readout
noise, the conversion in ADUs introduces rounding errors and have an incidence
on their statistics as we show next.

\subsection{Effects of digitization}
\label{sec:digitization}

The number $n_{\electron}$ of electrons transferred\footnote{$n_{\electron}$
accounts for the photo-electrons that are captured by the pixel well but also
for the electrons contributed by the \emph{dark current}; in what follows, the
electrons gained or lost during the transfer are assumed to be part of the
readout noise} by a given pixel to the analog to digital converter (ADC) is is
a random variable having a Poisson distribution of parameter $\mu =
\Expect{n_\electron}$ the mean number of electrons, $\Expect{\ldots}$ denoting
the expectation.  The randomness of $n_\electron$ is called the \emph{shot
noise} and, as explained before, is not the only source of noise or errors in
the values given by a detector.  To simplify the discussion, we call
\emph{readout noise} all random perturbations other than the shot noise.

To account for the various sources of noise, the digitized value may be assumed
to be given by:
\begin{equation}
  d = \Round*{(n_{\electron}/g) + z + \omega},
  \label{eq:ADC-model}
\end{equation}
where $z$ is the \emph{zero-level} (also called \emph{bias}) of the ADC, $g$ is
the \emph{gain} of the ADC, $\omega$ is a random variable accounting for the
readout noise and $\Round{\ldots}$ denotes conversion to an integer number of
ADUs.  The zero-level $z$ and the readout noise $\omega$ are expressed in ADUs
while the gain $g$ is in electrons per ADU ($\electron/\ADU$).

In Eq.~\eqref{eq:ADC-model}, $\Round{x} \in \Integers$ denotes the conversion
of the input value $x \in \Reals$ to some integer output value. Typically,
$\Round{x}$ rounds its argument to the nearest integer\footnote{a different
assumption is equivalent to changing the zero-level $z$} within some limits:
\begin{equation}
  \Round{x} = \min\Paren*{
    \max\Paren*{
      \Floor*{x + \textfrac12}, d_\Tag{min}
    }, d_\Tag{max}
  }
  \label{eq:digitization}
\end{equation}
with $\Floor{\ldots}$ the floor function and with $d_\Tag{min}$ and
$d_\Tag{max}$ the minimum and maximum output levels of the converter. Usually,
the clipping limits are $d_\Tag{min} = 0$ and $d_\Tag{max} = 2^{n_\Tag{bits}} -
1$ with $n_\Tag{bits}$ the number of bits encoding the output value. Note that
the upper clipping bound $d_\Tag{max}$ is not the same thing as the saturation
phenomenon which is due to the finite full-well capacity of the considered
pixel.  In what follows, we will focus on the consequences of the rounding
errors caused by the conversion to an integer and assume that clipping of
values does not occur. In the processing of the data, this amounts to imposing
that saturated pixels (whatever the reason) be considered as defective and
discarded.

The zero-level $z$ can take into account any constant bias so, without loss of
generality, we can assume that the readout noise is centered, \ie that
$\Expect{\omega} = 0$. \citet{Winick-1986-Cramer_Rao} assumes that the readout
noise be Poissonian as is the number $n_\electron$ of electrons so that, in
photon counting mode, the distribution of ADUs is Poissonian (and biased).  In
our model formalized by Eq.~\eqref{eq:ADC-model}, photon counting mode would
correspond to having $g=1$ and $z \in \Integers$.  Actual photon counting
detectors however involve light intensification (in ICCD, for \emph{Intensified
  CCD}) or electron multiplication (in EMCCD) and their output can have very
different statistics
\citep[\eg,][]{Thiebaut-1994-photon_counting_speckle_interferometry}.  Such
detectors are out of the scope of the present paper. Most other authors
\citep[\eg,][\etc]{Murtagh_et_all-1995-multiresolution,
  Foi_et_al-2008-Poissonian_Gaussian_noise_model_fitting,
  Chouzenoux_etal-2015-exact-Poisson_Gaussian_likelihood,
  Boulanger_etal-2018-nonsmooth_convex_optimization} assume that the readout
noise $\omega$ be Gaussian but do not consider digitization effects and model
the statistics of the ADUs as a mixture of Poisson and Gaussian noises.  To our
knowledge \oops{[check this or remove]}, there are no strong arguments in favor
of assuming that the exact statistics of the readout noise be Poissonian,
Gaussian, or something else.  Whatever this statistics may be, we show next
that the unavoidable digitization by the ADC introduces rounding errors which
rule out modeling the ADUs statistics as an \emph{exact} mixture
of Poissonian and, say, Gaussian noises.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/histograms}

\caption{Distributions of probabilities of the detector levels for a mean
number of electrons $\mu = 200\,\electron$ and variable gains $g \sim
3\,\electron/\ADU$. The zero-levels $z$ have been chosen to separate the
different distributions. \label{fig:histograms}}
\end{figure}

Neglecting clipping at $d_\Tag{min}$ and $d_\Tag{max}$ and assuming
$\Round{\ldots}$ rounds to the nearest integer toward zero in the
model~\eqref{eq:ADC-model}, reading the detector value $d$ knowing the actual
number $n_\electron$ of electrons implies that the readout noise realization
$\omega$ be in a given range:
\begin{equation}
  d - (n_\electron/g) - z - \textfrac12
  ≤ \omega <
  d - (n_\electron/g) - z + \textfrac12.
\end{equation}
Then, provided that the number of electrons $n_\electron$ and the readout noise
$\omega$ be independent and since $n_\electron$ has a Poissonian distribution of
parameter $µ$, the probability of the number of ADUs is given by:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr\nolimits_{D}(d)
  = \mathe^{-µ} \, \sum_{n ≥ 0} \frac{µ^n}{n!}
  \int_{d - (n/g) - z + 1/2}^{d - (n/g) - z - 1/2}
  \Pr\nolimits_{\Omega}(\omega)\,\mathd\omega,
\end{equation}
with $\Pr\nolimits_{\Omega}(\omega)$ the probability density function of
$\omega$.  Introducing $\Pr\nolimits_X(x)$ the probability density function
of $x = n_\electron + g\,\omega$ yields:
\begin{equation}
  \Pr\nolimits_{D}(d) = \int_{(d - z + 1/2)\,g}^{(d - z - 1/2)\,g}
  \Pr\nolimits_{X}(x)\,\mathd x.
  \label{eq:prob-ADU-exact}
\end{equation}
The distribution $\Pr\nolimits_X(x)$ is a mixture of a Poissonian distribution
and of the (scaled) distribution of the readout noise and may thus be a mixture
of Poissonian and Gaussian distributions if the readout noise is Gaussian.
Equation~\eqref{eq:prob-ADU-exact} however shows that, whatever the statistics
of the readout noise, $\Pr\nolimits_{D}(d)$ cannot just be the mixture of
Poissonian and Gaussian distributions as has been considered by some authors
\citep[\eg,][\etc]{Murtagh_et_all-1995-multiresolution,
  Foi_et_al-2008-Poissonian_Gaussian_noise_model_fitting,
  Chouzenoux_etal-2015-exact-Poisson_Gaussian_likelihood,
  Boulanger_etal-2018-nonsmooth_convex_optimization}: it would be at least
necessary to convolve this distribution by the response of the analog to
digital converter (ADC).  The effects of digitization can be illustrated under
the simplifying assumption of a negligible readout noise.

In the case of a negligible readout noise ($\omega \approx 0$), reading the
detector value $d$ implies that the actual number $n_\electron \in \Set{N}$ of
electrons is in a given range:
\begin{equation}
  d = \Round*{n_\electron/g + z}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  n_\electron \in \IntRange{n_\Tag{inf}(d;z,g), n_\Tag{sup}(d;z,g)}
\end{equation}
where for any integers $a$ and $b$ such that $a ≤ b$:
\begin{equation}
  \IntRange{a,b} \bydef \Brace*{n \in \Integers \SuchThat a ≤ n ≤ b},
\end{equation}
denotes a range of integers (we assume the range is empty if $a > b$) and
where: $n_\Tag{inf}(d;z,g)$ and $n_\Tag{sup}(d;z,g)$ are two integer-valued
functions of the number $d$ of ADUs knowing the zero-level $z$ and the gain
$g$:
\begin{align}
   n_\Tag{inf}(d;z,g)
   &= \max\Paren*{0, \Ceil*{\Paren*{d - z - \textfrac12}\,g}},\\
   n_\Tag{sup}(d;z,g)
   &= \Ceil*{\Paren*{d - z + \textfrac12}\,g} - 1,
\end{align}
with $\Ceil{\ldots}$ the ceiling function. Accounting for the Poissonian
distribution of the number $n_\electron$ of electrons, the probability of
getting a value $d$ knowing the mean number $µ$ of electrons, the zero-level
$z$ and the gain $g$ is given by:
\begin{align}
  \Pr\nolimits_{D}(d) = \begin{cases}
   \mathe^{-\mu} \,
   \sum_{n = n_\Tag{inf}(d;z,g)}^{n_\Tag{sup}(d;z,g)}
   \frac{\mu^n}{n!} & \text{$n_\Tag{inf}(d;z,g) ≤ n_\Tag{sup}(d;z,g)$}\\
  0 & \text{else.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:prob-ADU-no-readout-noise}
\end{align}
Figure~\ref{fig:histograms} shows this probability as a function of $d$ for
$\mu = 200\,\electron$ and various gains $g \sim 3\,\electron/\ADU$.

The mean number of levels that yields a given sensor value $d$ is equal to the
gain $g$ but the exact number of levels is given by:
\begin{equation}
  n_\Tag{lev}(d;z,g)
  = \max\Paren[\big]{0, n_\Tag{sup}(d;z,g) - n_\Tag{inf}(d;z,g) + 1}
\end{equation}
which, for given zero-level $z$ and gain $g$, depends on $d$.  Unless the gain
$g$ is integer, the uneven number of electrons that yields a given number of
ADUs leads to irregular distributions (moiré pattern) shown by
Fig.~\ref{fig:histograms}.  \oops{[The readout noise blurs these patterns.]}


\subsection{Approximation of the statistics of the ADUs}

Knowing the mean number of electrons $\mu$, the zero-level $z$, and the gain
$g$, the mean and variance of the measured number of ADUs are given by:
\begin{align}
  \Expect*{d}
  &= \sum_d d\,\Pr\nolimits_{D}(d),
  \label{eq:exact-detector-mean}
  \\
  \Var*{d}
  &= \sum_d \Paren[\big]{d - \Expect*{d}}^2\,
  \Pr\nolimits_{D}(d),
  \label{eq:exact-detector-variance}
\end{align}
where the probability $\Pr\nolimits_{D}(d)$ is given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:prob-ADU-exact} or Eq.~\eqref{eq:prob-ADU-no-readout-noise}
depending
on whether the readout noise is negligible. Although clipping has been
neglected to simplify this study, there are no closed form expressions for the
above statistical moments.  In what follows, we suggest a simple approximation
of the statistics of the number of ADUs.

\oops{[\citep{Foi_et_al-2008-Poissonian_Gaussian_noise_model_fitting} take
  clipping into account, we will advocate to discard saturated data.]}

Simple approximations of the expectation and variance of the number of ADUs can
be derived by neglecting the rounding errors due to the digitization:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:detector-statistics-with-no-rounding-errors}
\begin{align}
  \Expect*{d} &\approx
  \Expect*{n_\electron/g + z + \omega}
  = \mu/g + z,
  \label{eq:detector-mean-with-no-rounding-errors}
   \\
  \Var*{d} &\approx
  \Var*{n_\electron/g + z + \omega} = \mu/g^2 + \sigma^2,
  \label{eq:detector-variance-with-no-rounding-errors}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\sigma^2$ is the variance of $\omega$ the readout noise which, as done
before, has been assumed independent from $n_\electron$ the number of
electrons.

Figures~\ref{fig:means} and \ref{fig:variances} plot the differences between
the mean and the variance accounting and not accounting for rounding errors,
that is between Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-detector-mean} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:detector-mean-with-no-rounding-errors} for Fig.~\ref{fig:means}
and between Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-detector-variance} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:detector-variance-with-no-rounding-errors} for
Fig.~\ref{fig:variances}.  These figures show that there is a small variable
bias $\lesssim 0.005\,\ADU$ for the estimation of the mean given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:detector-mean-with-no-rounding-errors} while there is almost the
same bias for the variance given by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:detector-variance-with-no-rounding-errors}, except for small
integer gains $g=1$ (the photon-counting case), $g = 2$, or $3$.  The
variance bias can be approximately estimated assuming that rounding errors are
uniformly distributed on the range $[-\textfrac12,+\textfrac12]$.  Under this
assumption, the variance due to rounding errors has a closed form expression:
\begin{equation}
   \int_{-\textfrac12}^{+\textfrac12}u ^2\,\mathd u = \frac{1}{12}
   \simeq 0.083 \, \ADU^2,
   \label{eq:rounding-errors-variance}
\end{equation}
which is a good approximation of the variance bias in Fig.~\ref{fig:variances},
again except for small integer gains, say $g \in \Brace{1,2,3}$, because
rounding errors are far from being uniformly distributed for such gains.

Using the probability given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:prob-ADU-exact} is not very
convenient and we want to derive more practical approximations of the
distribution of the measured ADUs.  Approximating the sum in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:prob-ADU-no-readout-noise} by the mean number of terms, that is
$g$, times their value at the center of the integration interval, that is at
$n = (d - z)\,g$, yields:
\begin{align}
  \Pr\nolimits_{D}(d) \approx
  \frac{
    g\,\mathe^{-\mu}\,\mu^{(d - z)\,g}
  }{
    \Gamma\Paren*{(d - z)\,g + 1}
  },
  \label{eq:pdf-ADU-median-approx}
\end{align}
where $\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty \mathe^{-t}\,t^{x-1}\,\mathd t$ is the usual
gamma function.  This so-called \emph{central} approximation however also
amounts to neglecting the readout noise.  Another possibility is to approximate
the distribution of the ADUs in Eq.~\eqref{eq:prob-ADU-exact} by a Gaussian of
mean $\mu/g + z$ and variance $\mu/g^2 + \sigma^2$ which accounts for the
readout noise (and a possible variance bias) via the term $\sigma^2$.  This
approximation yields:
\begin{align}
  \Pr\nolimits_{D}(d)
  \approx \frac{
    \exp\Paren*{
      -\frac{\Paren*{d - \mu/g - z}^2}{2\,\Paren*{\mu/g^2 + \sigma^2}}
    }
  }{
    \sqrt{2\,\pi\,\Paren*{\mu/g^2 + \sigma^2}}
  }\,
  \label{eq:pdf-ADU-Gaussian-approx}
\end{align}
with $\sigma$ the standard deviation of other source of uncertainties than the
shot noise (\eg, the rounding errors and the readout noise). In the case of a
negligible readout noise, the two approximate distributions and the exact one
are shown by Fig.~\ref{fig:pdf-approx} with $\sigma^2 = 1/12$ to account for
rounding errors.  The two approximations appears as good as each other.  The
Gaussian approximation is however easier to handle and has the flexibility to
account for additional sources of noise (e.g., readout noise) via the
parameters $\sigma$ and $z$ (if these other contributions are not centered).

\section{Calibration of the detector}

Our model of the detector is specified by the Gaussian approximation in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pdf-ADU-Gaussian-approx} whose moments are given by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:detector-statistics-with-no-rounding-errors}.  As assumed by
this model, the behavior of each pixel of the detector is described by three
parameters: the gain $g$, the zero-level $z$ and the standard deviation
$\sigma$ of the readout noise.  We do not assume that these parameters shall be
the same for all the pixels: they are all pixel-wise quantities.  Hence and
further assuming that the noises are mutually independent between pixels, we
can tackle the problem of calibrating the parameters of the detector separately
for each of its pixels.  Unless otherwise specified, we thus consider in the
following a specific pixel out of the $N_\Tag{pix}$ pixels of the detector.

\oops{Clearly explain and justify that we consider any data not following the
  assumed model, notably saturated or defective pixels, as invalid data.}


\subsection{Model of the calibration data}

% This is routinely exploited to calibrate the parameters of the detector.  As
% explained in the following, this is also useful to extract the scientific
% signal in an optimal an consistent way.

\oops{Directly use source terms, no current terms.}

In order to extract the detector parameters, its behavior must be observed
under variable conditions.  This is customarily done by acquiring a number of
images with the detector for various exposure times and illuminations.  We
assume that other conditions that may affect the detector response (in
particular the temperature) can be considered as constant in the set of
calibration data.  We denote by $d_{k,\ell,n}$ the value of the considered
detector pixel in the $n$-th image acquired under $\ell$-th illumination
conditions, the supplementary index $k$ being to distinguish different exposure
times denoted by $\Delta t_{k,\ell}$.  Thus indices $(k,\ell)$ uniquely
identifies the observing conditions.  Under given such observing conditions,
the average number of electrons, say $µ_{k,\ell}$, scales linearly with the
exposure time $\Delta t_{k,\ell}$ as expressed by:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:temporal-behavior}
  µ_{k,\ell} = g\,c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell},
\end{equation}
where $c_{\ell}$ is a \emph{current} term which only depends on the
illumination conditions and hence on the index $\ell$.  In the above equation,
the current is directly the number of ADUs delivered per unit of exposure time
and is thus expressed in ADU per second ($\ADU/\second$).  Replacing
$µ_{k,\ell}$ by $g\,c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}$ in the expressions
\eqref{eq:detector-mean-with-no-rounding-errors} and
\eqref{eq:detector-variance-with-no-rounding-errors} of the expectation and of
the variance of the detector output yields:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:assumed-statistics}
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:assumed-expectation}
  m_{k,\ell} &\bydef \Expect{d_{k,\ell,n}}
               = c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + z,\\
               %= (\V h_\ell\T⋅\V s)\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + z,\\
  \label{eq:assumed-variance}
  v_{k,\ell} &\bydef \Var{d_{k,\ell,n}}
               = c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}/g + \sigma^2.
               %= (\V h_\ell\T⋅\V s)\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}/g + \sigma^2.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $m_{k,\ell}$ and $v_{k,\ell}$ are the mean and variance of the sub-set
$\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n}}_{n\in\IntRange{1,N_{k,\ell}}}$ of the values of the
considered pixel in the $N_{k,\ell}$ calibration images acquired under the same
illumination conditions which are characterized by the current $c_\ell$ and the
exposure time $\Delta t_{k,\ell}$.

In practice, the dark current and all sources seen by the detector contribute
to the number of electrons counted by the detector.  To reflect that, the
current term $c_\ell$ may be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sources-to-current-term}
  c_\ell = \V h_\ell\T⋅\V s
\end{equation}
where $\V s$ is a vector whose entries are the various possible \emph{source}
terms (the dark current, the thermal background, the illuminations by the
calibrations lamps, \etc) and $\V h_\ell$ is a vector of same size as $\V s$
and whose entries are either 1 or 0 depending on whether the sources are
\emph{on} or \emph{off} for the considered $\ell$-th illumination conditions.
The current and source terms are all expressed in ADU/s.  The relation between
these terms is summarized by the following equation:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sources-to-currents-model}
  \V c = \M H⋅\V s
\end{equation}
where $\M H$ is a known matrix whose rows are the $\V h_\ell$ vectors.

\subsection{Maximum likelihood calibration}

As may be clear from the preceding section, although there are only 3 parameters
per pixel (the zero-level $z$, the gain $g$ and the standard deviation of the
readout noise $\sigma$) to characterize the detector, calibrating these
parameters from empirical data introduces additional unknowns: the source terms
$\V s$. The calibration process turns out to require to estimate all these
unknowns, say $\V\theta=\Paren{z,g,\sigma,\V s}$, for each pixel.  Maximum
likelihood estimators are well known for their good properties \oops{[ref?]} and
our objective is to design a numerical method to find the maximum likelihood
estimator of our unknowns $\V\theta$ which is defined by:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:maximum-likelihood-parameters}
  \estim{\V\theta} = \argmin_{\V\theta\:\in\:\Omega}\lkl(\V\theta),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ denotes the set of acceptable parameters and:
\begin{align}
  \lkl(\V\theta)
  &= \zeta - 2\log\Pr(\V d\given\V\theta) \notag\\
  &= \sum_{k,\ell,n}\Paren*{
    \frac{\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n} -
        m_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)}^2}{v_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)}
    + \log\Paren*{v_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)}
  },
  \label{eq:cologlikelihood}
\end{align}
is the co-log-likelihood of $\V d$, the values of the considered pixel for the
whole calibration data-set, knowing the parameters $\V\theta$.  This expression
of $\lkl(\V\theta)$ follows from our assumption that the statistics of the data
delivered by a given pixel is approximately Gaussian and from the fact that
different calibration images are mutually independent.  In this expression,
$\zeta$ is a constant with respect to $\V\theta$ chosen to simplify the
expression of $\lkl(\V\theta)$.  The terms $m_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)$ and
$v_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)$ are the expectation and variance of the sub-set
$\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n}}_{n\in\IntRange{1,N_{k,\ell}}}$ knowing the parameters
$\V\theta$ which are obtained by combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:assumed-statistics}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:sources-to-currents-model}.

Introducing the empirical mean and variance of the sub-set
$\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n}}_{n\in\IntRange{1,N_{k,\ell}}}$:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:sample-mean}
  \empirical{m}_{k,\ell}
  &\bydef \frac{1}{N_{k,\ell}} \sum_{n=1}^{N_{k,\ell}} d_{k,\ell,n}, \\
  \label{eq:sample-variance}
  \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
  &\bydef \frac{1}{N_{k,\ell}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k,\ell}}
    \Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n} - \empirical{m}_{k,\ell}}^2,
\end{align}
the pixel co-log-likelihood can be simplified\footnote{replace
$\sum_n\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n} - m_{k,\ell}}^2$ by
$\sum_n\Paren*{\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n} - \empirical{m}_{k,\ell}} +
\Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}}^2$, then develop the squares and
simplify} to:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:factorized-cologlikelihood}
  \lkl(\V\theta) =
  \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
    \frac{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)}^2
    }{
      v_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)
    }
    + \log\Paren*{v_{k,\ell}(\V\theta)}
  }.
\end{align}
Hence the empirical mean $\empirical{m}_{k,\ell}$ and variance
$\empirical{v}_{k,\ell}$ provide \emph{sufficient statistics} for the considered
pixel in the $N_{k,\ell}$ data frames acquired with the same exposure time and
illumination.  Using this sufficient statistics and the pixel co-log-likelihood
$\lkl(\V\theta)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:factorized-cologlikelihood} can significantly
reduce the computational burden and the memory requirements for minimizing
$\lkl(\V\theta)$ in the parameters $\V\theta$.

Note that the empirical variance $\empirical{v}_{k,\ell}$ defined by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sample-variance} is a biased estimator of the
variance\footnote{by a factor $N_{k,\ell}/(N_{k,\ell} - 1)$}.  Also note that,
if only a single data frame has been acquired under $\ell$-th illumination and
with exposure time $\Delta t_{k,\ell}$, then $N_{k,\ell} = 1$,
$\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} = d_{k,\ell,1}$, and $\empirical{v}_{k,\ell} = 0$, but
has no other incidences on the method proposed to fit the parameters.

The standard deviation $\sigma$ accounts for all other noises than the shot
noise.  In particular, $\sigma$ accounts for the digitization errors.  Hence
assuming rounding to the nearest ADU implies that $\sigma ≥ \sigma_\Tag{min} =
1/\sqrt{12}$. Other constraints are that the source terms (the enlightenment
fluxes and the dark current) are necessarily nonnegative and that the gain is
strictly positive\footnote{in practice, the gain should not be less than one
electron per ADU}. Hence the set of acceptable parameters is:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:feasible-set}
  \Omega = \Brace*{
    \V\theta = \Paren{z,g,\sigma,\V s} \SuchThat
    \sigma ≥ \sigma_\Tag{min},\ s_{j} ≥ 0\ (\forall j),\ g > 0
  }.
\end{equation}

In spite of the fact that it is separable in pixels, the co-log-likelihood
$\lkl(\V\theta)$ is not trivial to jointly minimize in all the parameters
accounting for the constraints implemented by $\Omega$.  \oops{We propose in the
following a strategy to solve the problem by means of hierarchical optimization
and first show how to eliminate the bias and the gain of the problem thanks to
a simple change of variables.}


\subsection{Estimation of the zero level and of the gain}

As we shall see, it is convenient to replace the variance $\sigma^2$ of the
readout noise  by the parameter $\eta = g\,\sigma^2$.  As the gain is
necessarily strictly positive, the readout noise variance is always given by
$\sigma^2 = \eta/g$, so we can choose the unknown parameters to be:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:unknowns}
  \V\theta = (z, g, \eta, s_{1}, s_{2}, \ldots).
\end{equation}
The co-log-likelihood now write:
\begin{align}
  \lkl(\V\theta)
%  &= \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
%    \frac{
%      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
%      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
%    }{
%      u_{k,\ell}/g
%    }
%    + \log u_{k,\ell} - \log g
%  } \notag\\
  &= g\,\sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
  } - \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\log w_{k,\ell}\notag\\
  &\quad - N\,\log g + \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\log N_{k,\ell},
  \label{eq:rewritten-cologlikelihood}
\end{align}
with $N = \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}$ the total number of calibration images and:
\begin{align}
  %\label{eq:variance-times-gain}
  %u_{k,\ell}
  %&\bydef g\,v_{k,\ell} = c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta
  %= \V h_\ell\T⋅\V s\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta,\\
  \label{eq:reduced-precision-over-gain}
  w_{k,\ell} %&= N_{k,\ell}/u_{k,\ell}
  &\bydef \frac{N_{k,\ell}}{g\,v_{k,\ell}}
  = \frac{N_{k,\ell}}{c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta}
  = \frac{N_{k,\ell}}{\V h_\ell\T⋅\V s\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta},
\end{align}
which is the precision of the data-set
$\Paren*{d_{k,\ell,n}}_{n\in\IntRange{1,N_{k,\ell}}}$ times the gain $g$ and
which only depends on the unknowns $\eta$ and $\V s$.

A first interesting consequence of the chosen re-parametrization is that the
maximum likelihood zero level $z$ knowing the other parameters has a closed form
expression that does not depend on the gain $g$:
\begin{align}
  z^+\Paren*{\eta, \V s}
  &\bydef \argmin_{z} \lkl(\V\theta) \notag\\
  &= \argmin_{z} g\,\sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
  } \notag \\
  &= \argmin_{z} \sum_{k,\ell}
     w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2 \notag \\
  &= \frac{
      \sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,
      \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - c_\ell\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}}
    }{
      \sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}
    }
  \label{eq:marginal-bias}
\end{align}
which follow from the dependency of $m_{k,\ell}$ in $z$ expressed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:assumed-expectation}, from the facts that $w_{k,\ell}$ does not
depend on $z$, and that the gain $g$ is strictly positive.

% The expression $\V\theta\setminus\Brace{z,g}$ denotes the set of parameters
% $\V\theta$ minus the parameters $z$ and $g$.

Noting that neither $m_{k,\ell}$ and nor $w_{k,\ell}$ depend on the gain $g$
and that $\lkl(\V\theta)$ is strictly convex in $g$ (for $g > 0$), the best
gain knowing the other parameters has a closed form expression given by:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:best-gain-knowing-others}
  \argmin_{g} \lkl(\V\theta)
  = \frac{N}{
      \sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
        \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
        + \Paren[\big]{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
      }
    },
\end{equation}
which is obtained by solving $\partial\lkl(\V\theta)/\partial g=0$ in $g$.  The
right hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:best-gain-knowing-others} is always strictly
positive\footnote{This follows from, on the one hand, that
  $w_{k,\ell} = N_{k,\ell}/\Paren{\V h_\ell\T⋅\V s\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta} > 0$ because
  $\eta = g\,\sigma^2 ≥ g\,\sigma^2_\Tag{min} = g/12 > 0$ as $g > 0$, and
  because all entries of $\V h_\ell$ are nonnegative and the source terms
  $\V s$ are necessarily nonnegative; on the other hand,
  $\empirical{v}_{k,\ell} + \Paren[\big]{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} -
    m_{k,\ell}}^2$ is nonnegative by construction and can only be equal to zero if all
  measurements are exactly equal to their model!}.  Hence it is also the
minimum of $\lkl(\V\theta)$ in $g$ under the constraint that $g > 0$. Now since
$z^+$, the minimum of $\lkl(\V\theta)$ in $z$ does not depend on the gain $g$,
replacing the zero level $z$ by $z^+$ in the right hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:best-gain-knowing-others} yields the maximum likelihood estimator
of the gain knowing all other parameters than $z$ and $g$:
\begin{align}
  g^+\Paren*{\eta, \V s}
  &\bydef \argmin_{g > 0} \Brace*{\min_{z} \lkl(\V\theta)} \notag\\
  &= \frac{N}{
    \sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren[\big]{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m^+_{k,\ell}}^2
    }
  },
  \label{eq:marginal-gain}
\end{align}
with $N = \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}$ and:
\begin{equation}
  m^+_{k,\ell} \bydef c_\ell\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}
  + z^+\Paren*{\eta, \V s}
  = \V h_\ell\T⋅\V s\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + z^+\Paren*{\eta, \V s},
\end{equation}
which only depends on the unknowns $\eta$ and $\V s$.

Replacing these values of the zero level and of the gain in the
co-log-likelihood yields:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:marginal-cologlikelihood}
  \lklplus(\eta, \V s)
  &\bydef \min_{z,g > 0}\lkl(\V\theta) \notag\\
  &= \sum\nolimits_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{\log N_{k,\ell} - \log w_{k,\ell}}
     + N\,\Paren*{1 - \log g^+} \notag \\
  &= \xi - \sum\nolimits_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\log w_{k,\ell} \notag \\
  &\quad + N\,\log\Paren*{\sum\nolimits_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,
    \Paren*{\empirical{v}_{k,\ell} +
    \Paren[\big]{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m^+_{k,\ell}}^2}},
\end{align}
where $\xi = N - N\,\log N + \sum\nolimits_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\log N_{k,\ell}$
is a constant.

\subsection{Hierarchical optimization}

We have just shown that $\lkl(\V\theta)$ can be jointly minimized in $z$ and
$g$, so the problem reduces to:
\begin{equation}
  \estim{\eta}, \estim{\V s}
  = \argmin_{\eta > 0, \V s ≥ 0}
  \Brace*{\lklplus(\eta, \V s) \bydef \min_{g,z} \lkl(\V\theta)}.
\end{equation}

There is no closed form for the minimum of $\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$, but
starting from some initial guess $\eta\Iter0$ and  $\V s\Iter0$,
$\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$ can be minimized by a nonlinear multi-dimensional
optimization algorithm implementing bound constraints.  Instances of such
algorithms are \eg BOBYQA \citep{Powell-2009-BOBYQA} which is a derivative free
method and only requires to compute the criterion to minimize or VMLMB
\citep{Thiebaut-2002-optim_bdec} which is a quasi-Newton method which requires
to compute the criterion to minimize and its derivatives with respect to the
parameters.   Simplified expressions of the partial derivatives
$\partial\lklplus(\eta, \V s)/\partial\eta$ and
$\partial\lklplus(\eta, \V s)/\partial\V s$ of the reduced
co-log-likelihood $\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$ in the reduced parameters
$(\eta, \V s)$ are derived in
Appendix~\ref{sec:marginal-cologlikelihood-derivatives}.


\subsection{Other optimization methods}

Instead of the proposed hierarchical optimization strategy, there are other
possibility to find the maximum likelihood parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \oops{Iterative minimization of the co-log-likelihood.  One can consider
minimizing the co-log-likelihood $\lkl(\V\theta)$ in the parameters $\V\theta$.}

\item \oops{Iterative Reweighed Least Squares (IRLS).}

\item\oops{ Expectation--Maximization (EM)
\citep{Jezierska_et_al-2011-Poisson_Gaussian_noise}.}

\item \oops{Another possibility would be to marginalize out the source terms.
This is however no doable in practice, there are no closed form expression, and
there is some interest in estimating the source terms as well as the detector
parameters.  For instance the scientific data can be part of the processed data
set and the emission by the object of interest (disentangled form spurious
background emissions) could be then optimally extracted.}

\end{itemize}


\subsection{Initial solution}

Whatever the chosen non-linear optimization method, the problem being
non-convex \oops{[really?]}, providing a starting point not too far from the solution is of prime
importance.

\oops{The following initialization may be too complex, I would try a simpler
method: (i) the zero-level $z$ and the current / source terms can be initially
estimated by a simple least-squares fit by first assuming i.i.d.\ noise; (ii)
using a typical value for the gain $g\Iter0$ and for the standard deviation
$\eta\Iter0 = g\Iter0\,\sigma^2_\Tag{min}$, the zero-level $z$ and the
current / source terms can be improved by iteratively minimizing the criterion;
(iii) the criterion is minimized in all the parameters by iteratively minimizing
$\lkl(\V\theta)$ or by minimizing $\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$ (hierarchical
optimization).}

We note that if the zero-level $z$ and the ratio $\tau_{\ell} =
\eta/c_{\ell} > 0$ are assumed known, the \emph{current} terms $c_{\ell}$
are approximately solutions of simple independent linear least squares problems:
\begin{align}
  c^+_{\ell}(z,\tau)
  &= \argmin_{{\ell}}\lkl(\V\theta)
  \quad\text{s.t.\ $\eta = \tau\,c_{\ell}$}\quad \notag\\
  &\approx \argmin_{c ≥ 0}\sum_{k} \frac{
    \Paren*{d_{k,\ell} - c\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} -  z}^2
  }{
    \Delta t_{k,\ell} + \tau
  } \notag\\
  &\approx \max\Brace*{0, \frac{
    \sum_{k} \frac{
      \Paren*{d_{k,\ell} - z}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}
    }{
      \Delta t_{k,\ell} + \tau
    }
  }{
    \sum_{k} \frac{\Delta t_{k,\ell}^2}{\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \tau}
  }}.
  \label{eq:simple-c}
\end{align}
In practice, we choose the initial estimation of the zero-level for the
considered pixel to be the least value taken by this pixel for all frames:
\begin{equation}
  z\Iter0 = \min_{k,\ell} d_{k,\ell}.
  \label{eq:initial-bias}
\end{equation}
We further impose that $\tau_{\ell} = \tau$ be the same for all kinds
of illuminations $\ell$ and for all pixels $j$ and negligible compared to all
non-zero exposure times:
\begin{equation}
  \tau = \varepsilon_1\,
  \min_{\substack{
      k,\ell\\
      \Delta t_{k,\ell} > 0
  }} \Delta t_{k,\ell},
  \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
where  $\varepsilon_1 > 0$ is a small factor, and then choose the initial
\emph{current} terms according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:simple-c}:
\begin{equation}
  c\Iter0_{\ell} %= c^+_{\ell}\Paren[\big]{z\Iter0,\tau}
  = \frac{
    \sum_{k} \frac{
      \Paren*{d_{k,\ell} - z\Iter0}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}
    }{
      \Delta t_{k,\ell} + \tau
    }
  }{
    \sum_{k} \frac{\Delta t_{k,\ell}^2}{\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \tau}
  }.
  \label{eq:initial-c}
\end{equation}
To select the initial value $\eta\Iter0$ of $\eta$, we assume that the
readout noise is small for most cases and take the initial value to be the least
nonzero value of $c_{\ell}\Iter0\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}$ times a small factor
$\varepsilon_2 > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \eta\Iter0 = \varepsilon_2 \,
  \min_{\substack{
      k,\ell\\
      c_{\ell}\Iter0\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} > 0
  }} c_{\ell}\Iter0\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}.
  \label{eq:initial-beta}
\end{equation}
This choice ensures that $\eta\Iter0 > 0$.  Finally the initial estimate
of the
pixelwise gain is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:best-gain} but using the initial bias
instead of the best bias given $\V c\Iter0$ and $\eta\Iter0$:
\begin{equation}
  g\Iter0 = \Avg*{
    \frac{
      \Paren[\big]{
        d_{k,\ell} -c\Iter0_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} - z\Iter0
      }^2
    }{
      c\Iter0_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta\Iter0
    }
  }_{\!\!k,\ell}^{-1}.
  \label{eq:initial-gain}
\end{equation}
Thus the initial solution is determined by the choice of two small positive
constants.  In practice, we take $\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon_2 = 1/10$.

\begin{appendix}
\section{Computing the partial derivatives}
\label{sec:partial-derivatives}

In this appendix, we derive the partial derivatives of the co-log-likelihood
$\lkl(\V\theta)$ and the reduced co-log-likelihood $\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$
in the unknown parameters.

\subsection{Derivatives of the co-log-likelihood}
\label{sec:cologlikelihood-derivatives}

We first recall the expression of the co-log-likelihood given in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rewritten-cologlikelihood}:
\begin{align}
  \lkl(\V\theta)
  &= g\,\sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
  } - \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\log w_{k,\ell}\notag\\
  \label{eq:rewritten-cologlikelihood-appendix}
  &\quad - N\,\log g + \sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}\,\log N_{k,\ell},
\end{align}
with $\V\theta = (z, g, \eta, \V s)$ the unknown parameters, $N =
\sum_{k,\ell} N_{k,\ell}$ the total number of calibration images, and:
\begin{align}
  m_{k,\ell} &= c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + z,\\
  w_{k,\ell} &= \frac{N_{k,\ell}}{c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta},\\
  %c_\ell &= \V h_\ell\T⋅\V s,
  \V c &= \M H⋅\V s,
\end{align}
respectively given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:assumed-expectation},
\eqref{eq:reduced-precision-over-gain}, and
\eqref{eq:sources-to-currents-model}.  The reduced parameters are $(\eta, \V
s)$.  Other quantities, the number of images $N_{k,\ell}$ per sub-data-set, the
empirical mean $\empirical{m}_{k,\ell}$ and variance $\empirical{v}_{k,\ell}$
of the sub-data-sets, the exposure times $\Delta t_{k,\ell}$, and the sources
to currents vector $\V h_\ell$, are assumed known or do not depend on the
sought parameters.

From the expression of $\lkl(\V\theta)$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rewritten-cologlikelihood-appendix}, the partial derivatives of
the co-log-likelihood in the parameters can be computed by applying the chain
rule:
\begin{align}
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{z}
  &= \sum_{k,\ell}\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{m_{k,\ell}}\,\PDer{m_{k,\ell}}{z}
  = \sum_{k,\ell}\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{m_{k,\ell}},
  \\
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{g}
  &= \sum_{k,\ell} w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
  } - \frac{N}{g},
  \\
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{\eta}
  &=
  \sum_{k,\ell}\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{w_{k,\ell}}\,\PDer{w_{k,\ell}}{\eta}
  = - \sum_{k,\ell}\frac{w_{k,\ell}^2}{N_{k,\ell}}\,
      \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{w_{k,\ell}},
  \\
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{s_j}
  &= \sum_{\ell}\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{c_\ell}\,\PDer{c_\ell}{s_j}
   = \sum_\ell H_{j,\ell}\,\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{c_\ell},
\end{align}
where the following partial derivatives are needed:
\begin{align}
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{m_{k,\ell}}
  &= 2\,g\,w_{k,\ell}\,\Paren*{m_{k,\ell} - \empirical{m}_{k,\ell}},
  \\
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{w_{k,\ell}}
  &= g\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
    } - \frac{N_{k,\ell}}{w_{k,\ell}}\notag\\
  &=g\,\Paren*{
      \empirical{v}_{k,\ell}
      + \Paren*{\empirical{m}_{k,\ell} - m_{k,\ell}}^2
  } - \Paren*{c_{\ell}\,\Delta t_{k,\ell} + \eta},\\
  \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{c_\ell}
  &= \sum_{k}\Paren*{
    \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{m_{k,\ell}}\,\PDer{m_{k,\ell}}{c_\ell}
    + \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{w_{k,\ell}}\,\PDer{w_{k,\ell}}{c_\ell}
  }\notag\\
  &= \sum_k \Paren*{
      \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{m_{k,\ell}}
      - \frac{w_{k,\ell}^2}{N_{k,\ell}}\,\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{w_{k,\ell}}
  }\,\Delta t_{k,\ell}.
\end{align}


\subsection{Derivatives of the reduced co-log-likelihood}
\label{sec:marginal-cologlikelihood-derivatives}

We now consider the reduced co-log-likelihood
$\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:marginal-cologlikelihood} as:
\begin{equation}
  \lklplus(\eta, \V s) = \min_{z,g > 0}\lkl(\V\theta).
\end{equation}
To compute the partial derivatives of the reduced co-log-likelihood
$\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$ with respect to any parameter, say $\alpha$, we can
use the chain rule on the parameters $\V\theta = (z, g, \eta, \V s)$:
\begin{align}
  \PDer{\lklplus(\eta, \V s)}{\alpha}
  &= \Paren*{
    \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{z}\,\PDer{z}{\alpha}
  }_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+} + \Paren*{
    \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{g}\,\PDer{g}{\alpha}
  }_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+}\notag\\
  &\quad+ \Paren*{
    \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{\eta}\,\PDer{\eta}{\alpha}
  }_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+} +\Paren*{
    \sum\nolimits_{j}\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{s_j}\,\PDer{s_j}{\alpha}
  }_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+}
\end{align}
where the notation $\Paren*{\ldots}_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+}$ means to evaluate the
parenthesized expression for the parameters $\V\theta = \V\theta^+ = (z^+, g^+,
\eta, \V s)$ and where:
\begin{equation}
  z^+, g^+ = \argmin_{z, g > 0} \lkl(\V\theta)
\end{equation}
whose expressions are given by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:marginal-bias} and
\eqref{eq:marginal-gain} and which only depend on the reduced parameters
$(\eta, \V s)$.  By definition of $z^+$ and $g^+$ (and since the constraint
$g>0$ involves strict inequality), the partial derivatives of $\lkl(\V\theta)$
in $z$ and $g$ evaluated at $\V\theta^+$ are equal to zero:
\begin{align}
  z^+, g^+ = \argmin_{z, g > 0} \lkl(\V\theta)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \begin{cases}
  \Paren*{\partial\lkl(\V\theta)/\partial z}_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+} = 0\\[1ex]
  \Paren*{\partial\lkl(\V\theta)/\partial g}_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+} = 0\\
  \end{cases}
\end{align}
The partial derivative of the reduced co-log-likelihood $\lklplus(\eta, \V
s)$ then simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
  \PDer{\lklplus(\eta, \V s)}{\alpha}
  = \Paren*{
    \PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{\eta}\,\PDer{\eta}{\alpha}
  }_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+} + \Paren*{
    \sum\nolimits_{j}\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{s_j}\,\PDer{s_j}{\alpha}
  }_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+}.
  \label{eq:derive-reduced-cologlikelihood-in-alpha}
\end{equation}
According to this result, it is then sufficient to evaluate the above two
expressions for $z=z^+$ and $g=g^+$ to have the partial derivatives of the
reduced co-log-likelihood $\lklplus(\eta, \V s)$ in the reduced parameters
$(\eta, \V s)$:
\begin{align}
  \PDer{\lklplus(\eta, \V s)}{\eta}
  &= \Paren*{\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{\eta}}_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+}\\
  \PDer{\lklplus(\eta, \V s)}{s_j}
  &= \Paren*{\PDer{\lkl(\V\theta)}{s_j}}_{\V\theta=\V\theta^+},
\end{align}
using the derivatives derived in Appendix~\ref{sec:cologlikelihood-derivatives}.
